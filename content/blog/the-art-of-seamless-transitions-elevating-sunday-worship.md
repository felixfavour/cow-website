When a media volunteer sits down behind the computer on Sunday morning, they want one thing: clear direction. Too often, worship leaders assume the media booth can read minds. Then, a song repeats unexpectedly, an unannounced scripture is read, and the projection screen freezes on the wrong chorus.

A ten-minute email or message sent by Thursday evening fixes this. When you give your tech operators clear details about your plans, you take the pressure off their shoulders and help the congregation focus on worship. Here are eight specific things you should tell your church media team before Sunday morning.

### 1. The exact song roadmap and slide order

Do not just send song titles. A volunteer does not know if you plan to sing "Way Maker" with two verses, four chorus repeats, or an extended bridge tag. If your presentation software relies on jumping between slides manually, the operator has to guess where you are going next.

Send the exact roadmap in order. For example: Verse 1, Verse 2, Chorus, Verse 3, Chorus, Chorus, Bridge, Bridge, Chorus, Outro. If you build your slides in linear order—duplicating chorus slides so the operator only has to click the spacebar or right arrow—tell them. In presentation tools like Cloud of Worship, you can build your service schedule with the exact sequence so the operator simply advances slide by slide.

### 2. Scripture readings and the exact Bible translation

If you plan to read a passage of scripture between songs, do not read it from your phone without telling the tech team. Let them put it on the screen so the congregation can read along with you.

Specify three details: the exact reference, the verse range, and the translation you are reading from. Reading from the ESV while the screen displays the King James Version confuses the room. If you are using Cloud of Worship, the operator can use whole-Bible search to pull from versions like NIV, NLT, NKJV, or NASB in seconds, but they need to know which one you memorized or put in your notes.

### 3. Physical signals for spontaneous repeats

Worship leaders sometimes stay on a chorus or repeat a bridge when the moment calls for it. That is completely normal. What creates tension is when the media operator has no idea you are about to do it.

Give your media volunteer a simple, predictable physical cue during rehearsal. For instance: "If I raise my left hand flat with my palm facing the booth before the final line of the chorus, that means we are repeating the chorus one more time. If my hand stays down, we are moving to the bridge." Concrete signals stop the operator from jumping ahead to a verse you never intend to sing.

### 4. Non-standard lyrics or modified lines

Many classic hymns and modern worship anthems have alternate lines, removed verses, or updated phrasing. If your team sings a modernized version of "Amazing Grace" or skips verse three of "It Is Well," tell the media team in writing.

Give them the exact text edit: "In song three, verse two, line four: replace 'thy' with 'your'. Also, delete verse four completely." When you catch these variations during mid-week prep, your volunteer has time to update the song library and check line breaks instead of rushing to fix typos five minutes before the opening prayer.

### 5. What stays on screen during musical interludes

Media operators often panic when the music continues but nobody is singing. Should they leave the last chorus on screen? Should they clear the lyrics to show only the background video? Or should they blank the screen entirely?

Remove the guesswork. Give them rules for specific moments in your set. For example: "During the guitar solo in song two, keep the chorus lyrics on screen so people can keep reflecting. During the three-minute instrumental prayer after song four, clear the lyrics and leave just the moving background." Setting this expectation keeps the room looking clean.

### 6. Stage display requirements and countdowns

Your media volunteer is not only serving the congregation; they often run the stage display for the platform. Tell the booth what information the band needs to see and when.

If you use Cloud of Worship to feed a platform screen, let the operator know if you need the stage clock or a specific countdown timer running during soundcheck. Specify your preference: "Please make sure the stage display shows the next slide line and the current time, and start the five-minute countdown timer exactly at 9:55 AM so we hit the stage on time." Clear expectations keep the entire platform team aligned.

### 7. The exact verbal or musical cue that ends the set

Transitions out of worship often stall because the booth does not know who is speaking next or when the worship set is officially finished. Does the worship leader pray? Does the pastor walk up during the last chord? Does the screen need to switch directly to the sermon title slide?

Tell the media team your finishing cue: "I will play the final G-chord of the last song and say, 'Amen, church, let's welcome Pastor Mark.' That sentence is your cue to switch the stage display to the sermon timer and pull up the message title slide on the main screens." When the media team knows the trigger phrase, the handover happens on cue.

### 8. The schedule freeze deadline

Nothing stresses a volunteer media operator more than a setlist change sent at 7:30 AM on Sunday morning. Tech operators need time to review slide breaks, check contrast against video backgrounds, and ensure media assets play smoothly.

Agree on a hard deadline for service changes. For instance, establish that all setlists, lyrics, and scripture passages must be finalized by Friday at 3:00 PM. If an emergency change happens after the freeze, tell the team that you will walk to the media booth in person before rehearsal to explain the change face to face. Respecting their preparation time builds trust and long-term volunteer retention.
